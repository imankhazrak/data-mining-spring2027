Evaluating Classifiers

“96% accurate” can describe a useless model. Today we take a classifier’s output apart: which errors it makes, how the cutoff trades one error for another, and how ROC and lift charts summarise the whole trade-off in one picture.

In the bank’s validation set, 9.65% of customers accepted the loan. What accuracy does the naive rule “predict nobody accepts” achieve?

90.35%, with a sensitivity of 0. Any model must beat this benchmark on the metric that matters. With imbalanced classes, accuracy alone is nearly meaningless.

Push the cutoff down to 0.1: sensitivity soars, precision drops. Push it to 0.9: almost every predicted acceptor is real, but most acceptors are missed. No cutoff is “correct”; the right one depends on the cost of each mistake.

ROC curve and AUC

The ROC curve plots sensitivity against 1 − specificity for every possible cutoff. A perfect model hugs the top-left corner; a random one follows the diagonal. The area under the curve (AUC) condenses it into one number:

  • AUC = 0.5: no better than random ordering. AUC = 1: perfect separation.
  • Interpretation: the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative.
  • AUC is cutoff-free, which is great for comparing models and useless for choosing a cutoff.
Our model’s AUC is about 0.956. Which statement is correct?

C. AUC is about ranking, across all cutoffs. Accuracy and sensitivity belong to a specific cutoff.

Lift and cumulative gains

Often we can only act on a fraction of cases, such as mailing the top 10% of customers. Sort validation records by predicted probability, then ask: what share of all positives is captured in the top x%?

Top x% contactedShare of all acceptors capturedLift
5%50%9.9

30%94%3.1

Lift = (response rate in the selected group) ÷ (overall response rate). Contacting the model’s top 10% finds acceptors 7.6× as often as contacting 10% at random. Switch the demo’s right panel to “Cumulative gains” to see the curve.

A marketing budget allows 400 mailings out of 2,000 customers (20%). Using the model instead of random selection, about how many acceptors do you reach? (There are 193 in total.)

About 175 (91% of 193). Random mailing reaches only ≈ 39 (20%). That gap is the business value of the model, and it is a much more persuasive number for a manager than AUC.

Cumulative gains and decile lift

ord   <- order(prob, decreasing = TRUE)
yy    <- valid$Personal.Loan[ord]
gains <- cumsum(yy) / sum(yy)
plot(seq_along(yy) / length(yy), gains, type = "l", xlab = "Fraction contacted",
     ylab = "Fraction of acceptors captured"); abline(0, 1, lty = 2)
decile <- ceiling(10 * seq_along(yy) / length(yy))
barplot(tapply(yy, decile, mean) / mean(yy), names.arg = 1:10, ylab = "Lift", xlab = "Decile")

import numpy as np, pandas as pd, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
df = pd.DataFrame({"prob": np.asarray(prob), "actual": y_valid.values}).sort_values("prob", ascending=False)
df["gains"] = df["actual"].cumsum() / df["actual"].sum()
frac = np.arange(1, len(df) + 1) / len(df)
plt.plot(frac, df["gains"]); plt.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], "--"); plt.xlabel("Fraction contacted"); plt.show()
df["decile"] = np.ceil(10 * frac).astype(int)
(df.groupby("decile")["actual"].mean() / df["actual"].mean()).plot.bar(ylabel="Lift")
What you should see: the top decile has a lift near 7–8; by the 4th decile almost all acceptors are captured and lift falls below 1 after that.

Common pitfalls

  • Wrong positive class. R’s confusionMatrix defaults to the first factor level (“0”). Always set positive = "1".
  • Reporting accuracy alone on imbalanced data. Always show sensitivity and specificity, or precision, too.
  • Choosing the cutoff on the test set. Choose it on validation data, then report test performance at that cutoff.
  • Comparing AUCs from different validation sets. Compare models on the same records.
  • Confusing precision with sensitivity. “Of those flagged, how many are real?” is precision; “of the real ones, how many did we flag?” is sensitivity.
