Cluster Analysis

No outcome variable, no right answer, and still a lot to learn. Clustering groups records so that members of a group are alike and different groups are not. We will build groups two ways, hierarchically and with k-means, and learn to judge whether the groups mean anything.

2 sessions · ~140 minTextbook ch. 15

Concept map

Distance between records, and between clusters

Everything depends on how “similar” is measured. For numeric data, Euclidean distance on standardised variables is the default (unscaled, Sales in kWh would swamp everything else, as in L7–L8). Alternatives: Manhattan (sum of absolute differences, more robust), correlation-based distance (similar profiles), and matching or Jaccard coefficients for binary data.

Hierarchical clustering also needs a distance between clusters, the linkage:

LinkageDistance between clusters A and BTends to produce
Single (nearest neighbour)closest pair of membersLong chains; one giant cluster plus singletons
Complete (farthest neighbour)farthest pair of membersCompact clusters of similar diameter
Averageaverage over all cross-pairsA compromise
Wardincrease in within-cluster sum of squares when mergingCompact, similar-sized clusters (closest in spirit to k-means)

Hierarchical (agglomerative) clustering

  1. Start with each of the n records as its own cluster.
  2. Merge the two closest clusters (according to the linkage).
  3. Repeat until one cluster remains. The sequence of merges is drawn as a dendrogram.

Cutting the dendrogram at a height gives a clustering: every vertical line the cut crosses is one cluster.

Dendrogram of 22 U.S. utilities8 standardised financial variables · real linkages

Switch to single linkage at 4 clusters: chaining puts 19 of the 22 utilities in one group. Ward and complete give balanced, interpretable groups. Notice how the same data produce different answers. Clustering is a tool for exploration, not an oracle.

In a dendrogram, two utilities are joined at a very low height. What does that tell you?

B. Read similarity from the merge height, not from left-right position. Leaves next to each other on the axis can sit in very different branches.

k-means

k-means seeks k clusters that minimise the total within-cluster sum of squares (WSS):

\[ \text{WSS} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i \in C_j} \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_j \rVert^2 \]
  1. Choose k starting centroids (randomly, or with k-means++).
  2. Assign every record to its nearest centroid.
  3. Update each centroid to the mean of its records.
  4. Repeat 2–3 until assignments stop changing.
Step through k-meanssimulated customers

Alternate “assign” and “update” and watch WSS fall at every step: k-means can only improve, never get worse. Click “New random start” several times at k = 4 or 5: different starts can converge to different, worse solutions (local optima). That is why software runs many starts (nstart = 25, n_init = 25) and keeps the best.

Choosing k and judging clusters

  • Elbow plot: WSS always falls as k grows; look for the bend where extra clusters stop paying. For the utilities: k = 1: 176 · 2: 137 · 3: 107 · 4: 84 · 5: 71 · 6: 60.
  • Silhouette width: how much closer each record is to its own cluster than to the next one (−1 to 1; higher is better).
  • Stability: do the clusters survive a different random start, a different linkage or a bootstrap sample?
  • Interpretability and usefulness: can you name each cluster, and would a decision differ by cluster? This is the test that matters most.

plot(hc, hang = -1, cex = 0.8)
rect.hclust(hc, k = 4, border = 2:5)

from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, dendrogram, fcluster
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
L = linkage(z, method="ward")               # also "single", "complete", "average"
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 4)); dendrogram(L, labels=utilities.index.tolist(), leaf_rotation=90); plt.show()
memb = fcluster(L, t=4, criterion="maxclust"); print(pd.Series(memb).value_counts())

k-means, elbow, and cluster profiles

set.seed(1)
wss <- sapply(1:8, function(k) kmeans(z, centers = k, nstart = 25)$tot.withinss)
plot(1:8, wss, type = "b", xlab = "k", ylab = "WSS")    # elbow near 4
km <- kmeans(z, centers = 4, nstart = 25)
split(rownames(z), km$cluster)
aggregate(utilities[, -1], by = list(cluster = km$cluster), FUN = mean)   # profiles, original units
library(cluster); plot(silhouette(km$cluster, d))

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
wss = [KMeans(k, n_init=25, random_state=1).fit(z).inertia_ for k in range(1, 9)]
plt.plot(range(1, 9), wss, "o-"); plt.xlabel("k"); plt.ylabel("WSS"); plt.show()
km = KMeans(4, n_init=25, random_state=1).fit(z)
print(utilities.groupby(km.labels_).mean().round(2))                       # profiles, original units
for c in range(4): print(c, list(utilities.index[km.labels_ == c]))
print("silhouette:", round(silhouette_score(z, km.labels_), 3))

Common pitfalls

  • Not standardising: the cluster structure is then just the variable with the biggest units.
  • Trusting one run of k-means. Use many starts and check stability.
  • Reading left-right order in a dendrogram as similarity.
  • Clustering on irrelevant variables: every variable included shapes the distance. Choose variables for the purpose of the segmentation.
  • Treating clusters as real, natural categories. Clustering always returns clusters, even for structureless data.
