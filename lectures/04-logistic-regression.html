Logistic Regression

When the outcome is yes/no, a straight line is the wrong tool: it predicts probabilities below 0 and above 1. Logistic regression bends the line into an S-curve, gives every customer a probability, and lets us decide where to draw the line between “offer” and “don’t”.

Concept map

From probability to odds to logit

For a binary outcome we model \(p = P(Y = 1 \mid X)\). A linear model for \(p\) itself breaks the [0, 1] range, so we transform it twice:

ScaleDefinitionRangep = 0.10p = 0.50p = 0.90
Probability\(p\)0 to 10.100.500.90
Odds\(p/(1-p)\)0 to ∞0.1119
Logit (log-odds)\(\log\frac{p}{1-p}\)−∞ to ∞−2.2002.20

The logit can take any value, so it is safe to model it linearly. Solving back for \(p\) gives the logistic (S-shaped) function:

\[ \log\frac{p}{1-p} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_q x_q \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_q x_q)}} \]
A customer has a predicted logit of 0. What is their predicted probability of accepting the loan?

0.5. Logit 0 ⇔ odds 1 ⇔ even chance. Positive logits mean p > 0.5; negative logits mean p < 0.5.

Fitting by maximum likelihood

There is no closed-form least-squares answer here. Instead, logistic regression chooses the β’s that make the observed 0s and 1s most probable, maximising the log-likelihood

\[ \ell(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big[\, y_i \log \hat p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i) \,\big] \]

Each 1 wants its \(\hat p_i\) near 1; each 0 wants its \(\hat p_i\) near 0. The software searches iteratively for the best compromise.

Customers who accepted are plotted at the top (y = 1), the rest at the bottom (y = 0). Move β₁ and watch the log-likelihood: higher (closer to 0) is better. “Fit by MLE” runs gradient ascent from your current guess. The cutoff turns the curve into a decision: everyone right of the dashed line is classified “accept”.

Interpreting coefficients as odds ratios

A one-unit increase in \(x_j\) adds \(\beta_j\) to the logit, which multiplies the odds by \(e^{\beta_j}\). That multiplier is the odds ratio. Fitting on 3,000 training customers (Undergrad as the reference education level):

The odds ratio for Income is 1.06. A customer’s probability of acceptance is 0.40. If their income rises by $1k, their new probability is…

C. Odds = 0.4/0.6 = 0.667; × 1.06 = 0.707; p = 0.707/1.707 ≈ 0.414. Odds ratios act on odds, not on probabilities. The change in probability depends on where you start on the S-curve.

Watch out: Age and Experience

These two have r ≈ 0.99 in this data. Their coefficients come out with opposite signs (≈ −0.03 and +0.03) that roughly cancel. That is multicollinearity: fine for prediction, meaningless for interpretation. Drop one if you want readable coefficients.

From probabilities to classes

The model outputs \(\hat p\). To act, we pick a cutoff c and classify “1” when \(\hat p \ge c\). The default 0.5 is rarely the best choice when classes are imbalanced (only 9.6% of customers accepted) or when the two kinds of mistakes cost different amounts.

Lowering the cutoff from 0.5 to 0.2 lowers overall accuracy (95.9% → 94.5%). Could 0.2 still be the better business choice?

A. Accuracy treats every error the same. Lecture 5 gives us the tools (sensitivity, specificity, ROC, lift) to choose a cutoff that matches the decision, and Lecture 12 adds explicit costs.

Code walk: who will accept a personal loan?

Data: UniversalBank.csv: 5,000 customers, 480 accepted the last campaign’s offer.

Prepare

bank <- read.csv("data/UniversalBank.csv")
bank <- bank[, !(names(bank) %in% c("ID", "ZIP.Code"))]
bank$Education <- factor(bank$Education, levels = 1:3,
                         labels = c("Undergrad", "Graduate", "Advanced"))
set.seed(1)
train_idx <- sample(nrow(bank), 0.6 * nrow(bank))
train <- bank[train_idx, ]; valid <- bank[-train_idx, ]
mean(bank$Personal.Loan)          # 0.096

import pandas as pd, numpy as np
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
bank = pd.read_csv("data/UniversalBank.csv").drop(columns=["ID", "ZIP Code"])
bank["Education"] = pd.Categorical(bank["Education"].map({1: "Undergrad", 2: "Graduate", 3: "Advanced"}),
                                   categories=["Undergrad", "Graduate", "Advanced"])
X = pd.get_dummies(bank.drop(columns="Personal Loan"), drop_first=True, dtype=int)
y = bank["Personal Loan"]
X_train, X_valid, y_train, y_valid = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.4, random_state=1)
print(y.mean())                   # 0.096

Fit and read odds ratios

logit <- glm(Personal.Loan ~ ., data = train, family = "binomial")
summary(logit)
round(exp(coef(logit)), 3)        # odds ratios

import statsmodels.api as sm      # for a summary table with p-values
logit = sm.Logit(y_train, sm.add_constant(X_train)).fit()
print(logit.summary())
print(np.exp(logit.params).round(3))   # odds ratios
What you should see: positive, highly significant coefficients for Income, Family, CCAvg, CD Account and both education dummies (≈ +4.2 to +4.4); negative ones for Online, CreditCard and Securities Account.

Predict probabilities, then classes

prob <- predict(logit, newdata = valid, type = "response")   # type="response" gives p, not logit!
head(round(prob, 3))
library(caret)
for (cut in c(0.5, 0.2)) {
  pred <- factor(ifelse(prob >= cut, 1, 0), levels = c(0, 1))
  print(confusionMatrix(pred, factor(valid$Personal.Loan), positive = "1")$table)
}

from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, accuracy_score
prob = logit.predict(sm.add_constant(X_valid))
for cut in (0.5, 0.2):
    pred = (prob >= cut).astype(int)
    print(cut, accuracy_score(y_valid, pred)); print(confusion_matrix(y_valid, pred))
What you should see: with this split, cutoff 0.5 gives ≈ 96% accuracy but misses about a third of the acceptors; cutoff 0.2 catches ≈ 80% of them. Your exact counts depend on the split.

Common pitfalls

  • Forgetting type = "response" in R, which returns logits, not probabilities.
  • Reading β as a change in probability. It is a change in log-odds; \(e^\beta\) multiplies odds.
  • Defaulting to a 0.5 cutoff with imbalanced classes.
  • Perfect separation: if a predictor splits the classes perfectly, coefficients explode and warnings appear. Consider regularisation (L6).
  • Numeric codes for categories: Education 1/2/3 as a number assumes equal steps. Use a factor.
