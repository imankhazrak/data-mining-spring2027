Course description

Data mining is the set of tools, methods and theory for finding patterns in data and using them to predict, group and recommend. The course covers the complete workflow: exploring and preparing data, partitioning it honestly, fitting supervised models for numerical and categorical outcomes, evaluating them on data they have not seen, and using unsupervised methods when there is no outcome at all.

Methods include multiple linear and logistic regression, variable selection and regularization (ridge, lasso, elastic net), principal component analysis, k-nearest neighbors, classification and regression trees, neural networks, ensembles (bagging, random forests and boosting), strategies for rare events and imbalanced classes, cluster analysis, association rules and collaborative filtering. The emphasis is on predictive performance, judged on data the model has never seen, and on communicating results to people who will act on them.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course you will be able to:

  1. Frame a business or scientific question as a data-mining task (prediction vs. classification vs. clustering vs. association) and choose suitable methods.
  2. Prepare data for modeling: handle categorical predictors, missing values and scaling, and partition into training, validation and test sets without leakage.
  3. Fit and tune regression, classification and ensemble models in R and Python, using cross-validation to choose complexity.
  4. Evaluate models with the right metric: RMSE/MAE, confusion matrices, sensitivity/specificity, ROC/AUC and lift. Explain why accuracy can mislead on imbalanced data.
  5. Reduce dimension with variable selection, regularization and PCA, and explain the trade-offs.
  6. Discover structure without labels using hierarchical and k-means clustering, association rules and collaborative filtering.
  7. Communicate an end-to-end analysis in a reproducible report and an oral presentation.

Graded work

Homework (25%)

Five problem sets, one per unit, combining short conceptual questions with a data analysis. Submit one PDF or HTML report (rendered R Markdown/Quarto or a Jupyter notebook) and your source file on . Code that does not run, or results that cannot be reproduced from your file, earn partial credit at best. Deadlines are on the Assignments page.

Weekly practice quizzes (10%)

Twelve short quizzes on , each open Thursday through Sunday 11:59 PM. Two attempts are allowed and the higher score counts. Your lowest two quiz scores are dropped, which covers illness, travel and busy weeks, so there are no make-ups.

Midterm exams (2 × 20%)

    Exams are in class and closed-book, with one handwritten two-sided 8.5×11″ formula sheet allowed. They test concepts, interpreting software output and choosing methods. You will not write code from memory.

    Team project (25%)

    In teams of 3–4, you will take a real dataset of your choice from question to recommendation: exploration, at least three modeling approaches compared on held-out data, and a clear conclusion. Milestones are a proposal (3%), a progress report with EDA and a baseline (4%), a 12-minute presentation (6%), a final report with reproducible code (10%) and a peer evaluation (2%). Individual project grades may be adjusted based on the peer evaluation.

    Late work & make-ups

    • Homework: each student has three grace days for the semester, usable on homework only (at most two on any one assignment). After that, late homework loses 10% per day and is not accepted more than three days late. Grace days are applied automatically.
    • Project milestones: one day late −10%; not accepted after two days (except the final report, which cannot be late because grades are due).
    • Exams: a make-up is offered only for documented emergencies, university-sanctioned activities or religious observances, arranged before the exam whenever possible.
    • If something serious is affecting your work, talk to the instructor early. We can usually make a plan before things snowball.

    Attendance & participation

    Attendance is not graded directly, but the Thursday labs are where most homework skills are practised, and exams draw on the in-class examples. If you miss a class, work through that lecture’s companion page, which is designed to stand on its own.

    Academic honesty & generative AI

    All work must follow the BGSU Code of Academic Conduct. You are encouraged to discuss homework ideas with classmates, but what you submit (writing, code and figures) must be your own, and you must list anyone you discussed it with. Copying code or text from another student, past solutions or online sources without citation is a violation. Suspected violations are handled through the university’s academic honesty process.

    Generative AI policy

    You may use AI assistants (e.g. Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot) to explain concepts, debug errors and look up syntax, as you would use documentation. You may not use them to write your answers, interpretations or reports, or on exams and quizzes. If you use AI on an assignment, add a short note saying what you asked and how you used the answer. You are responsible for every line you submit: if you cannot explain it, it is not your work. [Instructor may adjust — TBD.]
